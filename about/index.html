How we know

Everything on this site comes from companies' own filings with the SEC. Nothing is estimated from news, and nothing is our opinion of anyone's performance.

Pay and shareholder returns

Since the 2022 proxy season, US companies have had to publish a “pay versus performance” table in their proxy statement: the CEO's reported pay for each recent year, the same pay with stock awards re-valued at year-end prices (“compensation actually paid”), and what $100 invested in the company at the start of the table would be worth at the end of each year. Companies tag those figures in machine-readable form; we read every company's figures from the SEC's XBRL data, then open the proxy behind each case we name and check that every figure shown is in that filing.

“Reported pay” is the Summary Compensation Table total: salary, bonus, stock and option awards at their value when granted, incentive pay and other compensation. A large grant counts in full in the year it's made. Years with two CEOs, and companies that tag the CEO by name, are tagged per person; we read those figures from the proxy itself, and where more than one person was CEO in a year, “CEO pay” combines them.

Tagged figures can carry the wrong scale: a company that tags dollars as thousands reports every figure 1,000 times too large. Where every year's average for the other executives is above $100 million, or every CEO figure is under $1,000, the company is held out of the lists and statistics until checked by hand.

For some companies the SEC's XBRL data never carries the table, even though their proxies tag it (Apple, Microsoft and Tesla among them). For every company with a public float of at least $500 million that's missing, we find its latest proxy since 2023 and read the tagged table straight from it.

The raise

For year-to-year changes we open every screened company's latest pay-versus-performance proxy and read who the CEO was each year from the filing's own tags. Only back-to-back years with the same CEO for both full years count, a new CEO's first year isn't used as the starting year, and pay under $100,000 is left out. The stock's return for a year comes from the same table's $100 figures. The full test, with standard errors and the same comparison against the year before, is in the methodology.

The ratio

Most US public companies must report the ratio of their CEO's annual pay to their median employee's. We read the passage from each company's latest proxy and keep a ratio only when the CEO pay and median pay printed beside it reproduce it. Companies choose how to find the median employee, and many count part-time and overseas staff, which pulls the median down.

Logos

Company icons identify companies; they come from each company's own website (found through Wikidata or the company's proxy) or Wikimedia Commons, and belong to their owners. Companies without one get a lettered badge.

Exit pay and careers

Golden parachutes and failing-up cases are researched filing by filing: separation agreements, 8-K announcements, the proxy after a departure, each company's own stock-performance tables. Every number is quoted from a filing and the quote is machine-checked against it.

What these numbers don't say

A falling stock has many causes: markets, industries, decisions made before a person arrived. We show what shareholders got while a person was paid; we don't claim that person caused it. Where a company's own peer group fell too, we show that alongside. The data, code and full methodology are on GitHub.
